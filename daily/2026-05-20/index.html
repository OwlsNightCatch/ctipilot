    Previously unknown flaw in Huawei enterprise router software caused POST Luxembourg's nationwide telecom outage of July 2025; no CVE ten months later

    An attack exploiting a previously unknown vulnerability in Huawei enterprise router software caused POST Luxembourg's nationwide outage on 23 July 2025, according to multiple sources briefed on the matter, disrupting mobile, landline and emergency communications for more than three hours (The Record, 2026-05-19). POST's head of communications Paul Rausch called it a denial-of-service attack on a network device that exploited "a non-public, non-documented behaviour, for which no patch was available at the time", and anonymous sources described it as a zero-day attack (The Record, 2026-05-19). Specially crafted network traffic sent the Huawei enterprise routers into a continuous restart loop: the public prosecutor said corrupted data relayed through POST in its role as internet service provider made its systems stop and reboot instead of relaying it, and a spokesperson for the High Commission for National Protection said investigators found "no evidence that an attack was specifically directed at POST Luxembourg as a chosen target" (The Record, 2026-05-19). POST said neither of the earlier, publicly disclosed denial-of-service flaws in Huawei's VRP operating system was involved, Huawei told POST it had never seen the attack among its customers, and no CVE or public warning has been issued (The Record, 2026-05-19).

    Exposure
    Operators running Huawei enterprise routers; it remains unclear whether the flaw was ever fully patched or whether similar Huawei systems remain vulnerable, and Huawei publishes enterprise advisories only through a restricted customer portal (The Record, 2026-05-19).
    Detection
    Network device health and availability telemetry: the failure was routers repeatedly stopping and rebooting on transit traffic they should have forwarded, and POST clarified that it was not a volumetric DDoS (The Record, 2026-05-19).
    Defender takeaway
    With no CVE or public advisory to patch against, an operator of Huawei enterprise routers has to ask Huawei, through the restricted customer portal or its account team, whether its router software carries a fix for the Luxembourg failure (The Record, 2026-05-19); without Huawei routers in the network there is no decision to make.

    Microsoft DCU disrupts Fox Tempest, a malware-signing service that enabled Rhysida deployments and is linked to INC, Qilin and Akira affiliates

    Microsoft's Digital Crimes Unit unsealed a legal case in the US District Court for the Southern District of New York against Fox Tempest, a malware-signing-as-a-service (MSaaS) operation active since May 2025. It seized the service's website, took hundreds of the virtual machines running the operation offline and blocked access to a site hosting its code (Microsoft On the Issues, 2026-05-19). Fox Tempest abused Microsoft Artifact Signing (formerly Azure Trusted Signing) to obtain Microsoft-issued code-signing certificates valid for only 72 hours, which let malware masquerade as software such as AnyDesk, Teams, PuTTY and Webex and bypass security controls. Microsoft assesses that the actor very likely used stolen identities based in the United States and Canada to pass Artifact Signing's identity validation (Microsoft Threat Intelligence, 2026-05-19).

    Customers uploaded files through a web portal and, from February 2026, through pre-configured virtual machines hosted on Cloudzy infrastructure, and received signed binaries in return. They chose plans priced at $5,000, $7,500 or $9,000, with higher plans getting priority in the queue (Microsoft Threat Intelligence, 2026-05-19). Microsoft has revoked over a thousand certificates attributed to Fox Tempest (Microsoft Threat Intelligence, 2026-05-19) and reports the operators trying to move operations and customers to another code-signing service (Microsoft On the Issues, 2026-05-19).

    Microsoft links Fox Tempest to Vanilla Tempest, Storm-0501, Storm-2561 and Storm-0249, which all used Fox Tempest-signed malware in active intrusions delivered through purchased advertisements, malvertising and SEO poisoning. Separately, cryptocurrency analysis ties the actor to affiliates delivering the INC, Qilin and Akira ransomware families (Microsoft Threat Intelligence, 2026-05-19). In Microsoft's case study, Vanilla Tempest bought ads that sent users searching for Microsoft Teams to fraudulent download pages serving a signed, trojanized Teams installer, which deployed the Oyster backdoor (also known as Broomstick) and in some cases Rhysida ransomware. Microsoft lists healthcare, education, government and financial services among the affected sectors, and the United States, France, India and China among the affected countries (Microsoft Threat Intelligence, 2026-05-19).

    Exposure
    Users who fetch AnyDesk, Teams, PuTTY or Webex installers from search results or ads rather than from the vendor, the delivery route Microsoft describes for Fox Tempest-signed malware (Microsoft Threat Intelligence, 2026-05-19).
    Detection
    Web proxy and DNS telemetry of installer downloads for those tools from domains that are not the vendor's, followed by endpoint telemetry of the installer establishing persistence and outbound command-and-control, the behavior Microsoft describes for the Oyster backdoor (Microsoft Threat Intelligence, 2026-05-19).

    Triage: a genuine Teams, AnyDesk, PuTTY or Webex installer carries its publisher's signature. A Fox Tempest-signed copy carries a valid Microsoft-issued Artifact Signing certificate in the name of an entity that is not the product's publisher, obtained, Microsoft assesses, with stolen identities (Microsoft Threat Intelligence, 2026-05-19).

    Defender takeaway
    The takedown removes one signing service, not the technique, and the operators are already moving to another (Microsoft On the Issues, 2026-05-19). Trust a signed remote-access or collaboration installer only when the signer is the product's publisher.

    actions-cool/issues-helper GitHub Action compromised in Mini Shai-Hulud: 53 tags moved to imposter commits that read Runner.Worker memory, re-enabled in September with the tags intact

    StepSecurity disclosed on 2026-05-18 that every existing tag of the actions-cool/issues-helper GitHub Action, 53 in total, had been moved to its own imposter commit that is not reachable from the action's default branch, and that all 15 tags of the companion actions-cool/maintain-one-comment action were moved the same way (StepSecurity, 2026-05-18). The payload downloads the Bun JavaScript runtime to the runner, then a python3 child process reads the memory of the Runner.Worker process, which holds the workflow's decrypted secrets, filters the captured bytes for values flagged as secret and sends them over HTTPS to an attacker-controlled domain (StepSecurity, 2026-05-18). The 53 imposter commits carry fake "Build action for vX.Y.Z" messages in the maintainer's style and were all created within 3 minutes 16 seconds on 2026-05-18 (StepSecurity, 2026-05-18). Socket's head of threat intelligence told The Hacker News that the @antv npm compromise in the Mini Shai-Hulud campaign is likely linked to the actions-cool hack because the two share an exfiltration domain, and that "the overlap is strong enough that we're treating them as related" (The Hacker News, 2026-05-19). GitHub's security team disabled both repositories on 2026-05-19 but left the malicious tags in place; both became reachable again on 2026-09-16, so every workflow referencing either action by tag ran the payload again until GitHub disabled them a second time by 2026-09-25 (Socket, 2026-09-24). SafeDep found six more repositories infected through issues-helper between 2026-09-20 and 09-24, with the payload also committing Claude Code and VS Code hooks that run on a developer's machine when the project is opened (The Hacker News, 2026-09-25).

    Exposure
    Any workflow that referenced either action by a version tag pulled the malicious code on its next run after the tags moved; "Only workflows pinned to a known-good full commit SHA are unaffected" (StepSecurity, 2026-05-18). That happened on 2026-05-18 to 05-19 and again from 2026-09-16 until the second disable by 2026-09-25, and for issues-helper alone GitHub's dependency graph lists about 15,000 dependent repositories (Socket, 2026-09-24).
    Detection
    On the runner, the Bun runtime appearing under /home/runner/.bun/bin/bun, a python3 child process reading /proc/<Runner.Worker PID>/mem, gh auth token and sudo python3 executions, tr and grep pipelines filtering for secret-flagged values, and an outbound HTTPS connection to a domain outside the workflow's normal destinations (StepSecurity, 2026-05-18). In workflow definitions, an action reference that resolves to a commit matching no legitimate tag or branch head of the action's repository is the signature of this attack (StepSecurity, 2026-05-18). In workflow run history, runs of either action that start succeeding after a stretch of job-setup failures, or whose duration jumps from seconds to minutes, with runner logs showing Bun being set up for the action (Socket, 2026-09-24); in repository history, commits from 2026-09-16 onward that nobody can account for (Socket, 2026-09-24), in particular added Claude Code or VS Code hook configuration (The Hacker News, 2026-09-25).

    Triage: an issue-housekeeping action has no reason to install a separate JavaScript runtime or run a multi-minute script; a scheduled workflow that switches from failing in seconds to running for minutes without any change to its own code means the tagged action became downloadable again (Socket, 2026-09-24).

    Defender takeaway
    Treat any workflow run that used either action by tag on 2026-05-18 to 05-19 or from 2026-09-16 to 09-25 as a secrets exposure: rotate every secret the workflow could read, review what its GitHub token was allowed to do, audit the repository for commits from 2026-09-16 onward, and remove the actions or pin them to a full commit SHA verified to predate 2026-05-18 (Socket, 2026-09-24). Disabling a repository leaves its tags untouched, so a later re-enable reactivates the compromise without any change to your own workflow file; SHA pinning removes that dependency (Socket, 2026-09-24). Runner egress control is the layer that still holds when a compromised action runs: StepSecurity added the exfiltration domain to Harden-Runner's global block list, which blocks outbound connections to it even in audit mode, so that "even if a compromised action somehow runs, the credentials cannot leave the runner" (StepSecurity, 2026-05-18).

    Storm-2949: SSPR abuse of four privileged users turned into a data-theft sweep across Microsoft 365, Key Vault, SQL, Storage and Azure VMs

    Microsoft Threat Intelligence published its account of an intrusion by Storm-2949 on 2026-05-18: a campaign whose singular focus was to exfiltrate as much sensitive data as possible from one target organization's high-value assets, across Microsoft 365, file hosting and the Azure production environment (Microsoft, 2026-05-18). The actor did not rely on traditional malware but on legitimate cloud and Azure management features that blend into administrative behavior (Microsoft, 2026-05-18). Storm is Microsoft's temporary designation for activity not yet classified, and Microsoft saw no follow-on extortion and no link to ShinyHunters (BleepingComputer, 2026-05-19).

    Identity takeover. Microsoft assesses with high confidence that Storm-2949 abused Self-Service Password Reset (SSPR): the actor starts a reset on a targeted user's behalf and persuades the user to approve the resulting MFA prompts, likely through an organized and convincing phishing scheme, for example by posing as IT support (Microsoft, 2026-05-18). It then reset the password, removed the user's phone, e-mail and Authenticator methods and enrolled Microsoft Authenticator on its own device, locking the user out; the victims included IT personnel and senior leadership, and the actor repeated the process until it held four accounts (Microsoft, 2026-05-18).

    Directory discovery and Microsoft 365 theft. A custom Python script enumerated users and applications through Microsoft Graph to find privileged identities, and an attempt to add credentials to a service principal failed for lack of permissions (Microsoft, 2026-05-18). The actor searched OneDrive and SharePoint for VPN configuration and remote-access documents and in one instance downloaded thousands of files from the OneDrive web interface in a single action (Microsoft, 2026-05-18).

    Azure control plane. With privileged custom RBAC roles on several subscriptions, the actor called microsoft.Web/sites/publishxml/action to retrieve the publishing profiles of secondary App Service apps, which often hold FTP, Web Deploy and Kudu credentials; with an Owner role over one Key Vault it changed the vault's access configuration and read dozens of secrets within four minutes; Microsoft believes they held the credentials the actor then used to take over the primary production web app and change its password (Microsoft, 2026-05-18). It opened SQL server firewall rules with microsoft.sql/servers/firewallrules/write and deleted them after taking data, opened storage account network access to its own addresses with microsoft.storage/storageaccounts/write, and retrieved account keys and SAS tokens with microsoft.Storage/storageAccounts/listkeys/action to download blobs over several days with an Azure SDK script (Microsoft, 2026-05-18).

    Virtual machines and endpoints. Through the VMAccess extension the actor created a local administrator on a VM, and through Run Command it tried to use the VM's managed-identity token from the Instance Metadata Service against Key Vault, which failed on permissions (Microsoft, 2026-05-18). A Run Command PowerShell script then attempted to weaken Defender Antivirus by disabling several protections, installed ScreenConnect from actor infrastructure under names resembling Windows components, and attempted to clear event logs, command history and temporary files; over ScreenConnect the actor ran host and domain discovery, exfiltrated .pfx certificate files and scanned file shares for password strings (Microsoft, 2026-05-18).

    Exposure
    Entra ID tenants where users with privileged Azure RBAC roles over Key Vault, App Service, SQL, Storage or VMs can reset their password through SSPR; Microsoft names no sector, country or victim (Microsoft, 2026-05-18).
    Detection
    Identity audit logs first: Entra ID records reset and registration activity in the Self-Service Password Management audit category, and combined security-information registration events, such as a new Authenticator registration, under Security > Authentication Methods (Microsoft Learn); the pattern here is a reset followed by removal of every existing method and a new Authenticator registration (Microsoft, 2026-05-18). Then the Azure activity log for the operations above from one principal (publishing-profile retrieval, Key Vault access changes, SQL firewall and storage network rule writes, key listing, VMAccess and Run Command), and on VMs the disabling of Defender protections, a new remote-access service and cleared event logs (Microsoft, 2026-05-18). Evicting the accounts is not enough: the actor's Authenticator registrations, the web app password it changed, the Key Vault secrets it read and the storage keys it listed all stay usable until they are removed or rotated.
    Defender takeaway
    Require phishing-resistant MFA for administrators and privileged users and make sure every privileged user already has a registered MFA method, which blocks the malicious registration step; limit publishing-profile access, Run Command and VMAccess through RBAC, and prefer Azure RBAC over Key Vault access policies (Microsoft, 2026-05-18).
