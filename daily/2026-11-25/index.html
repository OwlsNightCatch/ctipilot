    BACS: first confirmed cyberattacks carried out with AI agents against Swiss companies and critical-infrastructure operators, five confirmed cases according to its director, three on critical-infrastructure operators; internet-reachable agents such as ARTEX and CyberStrikeAI have almost doubled since October

    BACS reported on 2026-11-24 that in recent days it had received its first reports of cyberattacks against organisations in Switzerland carried out with the support of an AI agent, that the victims are various companies and operators of critical infrastructure in at least two sectors, and that in every case known to it then the use of AI agents led to a successful compromise of the victim's IT systems (BACS, 2026-11-24). Its director told SRF on 2026-11-26 that BACS has five confirmed cases, three on critical-infrastructure operators, with SMEs that reported voluntarily also hacked, and SRF reports that systems were compromised in four of the five (translated from German) (SRF, 2026-11-26); RTS reads three critical-infrastructure attacks of which two succeeded, without saying whether the count covers all five (translated from French) (RTS, 2026-11-26). BACS calls these the first confirmed Swiss cases and says the attackers used so-called "Agentic Offensive Frameworks", agents that independently search for weaknesses, choose attack tools, evaluate the results and plan the next steps (BACS, 2026-11-24). Neither the page nor the two outlets name a framework used in these cases, a victim sector or an entry point; SRF reports no larger outages and an unknown actor, and BACS shares technical indicators for detecting such AI tools with critical-infrastructure operators (SRF, 2026-11-26; BACS, 2026-11-24). Netzwoche reported on 2026-11-02 that BACS's GovCERT co-head had said at the Gohack26 symposium that no case in which autonomous AI agents hacked someone had been reported to the office so far (translated from German) (Netzwoche, 2026-11-02).

    Describing the tool class in general, BACS says such agents exploit, for example, standard or easily guessed passwords and known vulnerabilities in software or internet-connected systems, often need only a fraction of the time a human would to find and exploit vulnerable systems, and switch to other known attack methods when they meet a defence (BACS, 2026-11-24). It adds that the number of internet-reachable AI agents such as ARTEX or CyberStrikeAI has almost doubled since October 2026, with no baseline or counting method given, names the two tools only as examples and does not say they were used in the Swiss cases, and expects an accumulation of fast, successful attacks (BACS, 2026-11-24).

    Exposure
    Internet-reachable systems with standard or easily guessed passwords or with known, unpatched vulnerabilities, which is what BACS says such agents exploit; BACS addresses its recommendations to companies in Switzerland and does not name administrations or sectors.
    Detection
    BACS recommends detecting when identities, applications, workloads and infrastructure deviate from their established behaviour patterns (BACS, 2026-11-24); BACS says it shares technical indicators with critical-infrastructure operators and publishes none in the text.
    Defender takeaway
    BACS asks that critical security updates for internet-reachable systems be installed as soon as possible and at the latest after 24 hours (the BACS German text gives no start point; the official English page and SRF's report say after release) (BACS, 2026-11-24; SRF, 2026-11-26), that access to exposed systems be limited by allow-list or geo-blocking, and that MFA or passkeys cover every internet-reachable internal resource without exception (BACS, 2026-11-24). Check the internet-facing estate for default and guessable passwords and for patch lag against that 24-hour ceiling first.

    GreyNoise GT13: an unattributed actor that GreyNoise believes used LLM tooling held persistent access to at least 24 Check Point Security Management servers at 22 organisations, government among them, using CVE-2026-16232 and a Rejetto HFS relay hijacked through CVE-2026-61500

    GreyNoise reports an intrusion campaign it tracks as GT13 against Check Point Security Management servers: at least 22 organisations and 24 servers, confirmed activity against the Check Point servers from 2026-10-17 to 2026-11-02 and the actor's relay observed from 2026-10-02, victims in 17 named countries and territories (Germany, Austria and France among them, Switzerland not named) and sectors including government, financial services, energy and transportation (GreyNoise, 2026-11-23). GreyNoise could not determine when or how the actor first reached the Check Point servers, only that it did so before compromising the Rejetto HFS server it used as a relay; it does not attribute GT13, cannot judge motive and believes that the actor used LLM and agentic tooling (GreyNoise, 2026-11-23).

    The actor ran a script for the SmartConsole authentication bypass CVE-2026-16232, purportedly adapted from the Metasploit module, to launch more than 100 management tasks, among them copying the firewall database and the internal certificate authority, minting a certificate and unloading security policies, which would disable the firewall (GreyNoise, 2026-11-23). Check Point confirmed the use of CVE-2026-16232 and called the flaw and its protections not new (BleepingComputer, 2026-11-24); the fixes are Jumbo Hotfix Accumulator R82.10 from Take 36, R82 from Take 118 and R81.20 from Take 158, and exploitation needs internet access to the management server with no restriction on Trusted Clients (Check Point, 2026-09-24); Check Point's disclosure table scores the flaw 9.3 and lists exploitation against a handful of customers with a specific configuration (Check Point, 2026-07-22). The relay was taken over by forging an HFS session cookie after recovering the signing key from login responses (CVE-2026-61500, HFS 3.0.0 to below 3.2.1) (GreyNoise, 2026-11-23; VulnCheck, 2026-07-13).

    The actor planted an SSH key on its relay, and GreyNoise concludes from scp deliveries that each compromised device holds the matching private key; it also ran Nmap and a Chisel reverse proxy, used at least 30 Python scripts with victim-themed credential lists, and probed NetScaler, Ivanti Connect Secure, vCenter, Exchange, Oracle TNS assets and domain controllers without any stated success. A bulk certificate task to 24 servers failed on each; GreyNoise notified victims directly or through government partners and expects the campaign to be ongoing (GreyNoise, 2026-11-23).

    Exposure
    Any Check Point Security Management or Multi-Domain Security Management server on R82.10, R82, R81.20 or an unsupported earlier train, below the fixed Jumbo Hotfix Accumulator, whose Trusted Clients list was unrestricted, reached from the internet or from a compromised internal host.
    Detection
    In management-server audit logs, SmartConsole sessions authenticated by "Password based application token", which Check Point says to treat as suspicious and check for an unrecognised administrator name or address (Check Point, 2026-09-24); script-run, certificate-management and policy-unload events; outbound SSH or scp from management servers to the relay and other non-administrator hosts; unfamiliar SSH private keys and a registration in /etc/ld.so.preload, which GreyNoise says may indicate an implant; tunnelling-tool process creation. A server patched later still needs this check, reaching back to at least early October, since the actor held persistent access and copied the internal CA.
    Defender takeaway
    Install the fixed Jumbo Hotfix Accumulator and restrict Trusted Clients now, then treat any server with an unexplained application-token login as a compromised management plane: review policy changes and rebuild trust where the internal CA or stored credentials left.

    Unit 42: more than ten campaigns in July to September 2026 ran autonomous AI agents from web-shell footholds on vulnerable web applications, with a command-gap median of 1.4 seconds, ===[Title]=== script headers and 40 to 100 unique Base64 payloads per session

    Unit 42 tracked more than ten global campaigns between July and September 2026 in which different attackers deployed autonomous AI agents, against sectors including government, logistics, manufacturing, agriculture and education. Every campaign began with a web shell deployed through a vulnerable web application, typically supplied by the human operator, after which the agent did reconnaissance, credential harvesting, privilege escalation and lateral movement; Unit 42 names no product, CVE or flaw class for the entry vectors and says the novelty was speed and efficiency, not technique (Unit 42, 2026-11-24).

    The traces: a median gap of 1.4 seconds between commands once an agent took over, against 23.9 seconds in the human phase of a subset of attacks; about 800 unique commands in 140 minutes in one case; section headers such as ===[Title]=== in shell commands and scripts in every campaign and step-by-step reasoning notes in more than half; 40 to 100 unique, target-tailored Base64 payloads per session; and no compiled implants, the arsenal being in most cases simple single-use scripts such as PHP web shells, PowerShell WMI backdoors and Python credential harvesters. The agents probed defences: the same find command ran on six endpoints excluding three security vendors, and one agent applied strace to the Microsoft Defender for Endpoint daemon (Unit 42, 2026-11-24).

    The Copy Fail exploit recurred across several organisations; in a different attack sequence an agent tried the public PwnKit and CVE-2024-1086 binaries, then wrote custom C on the target and rewrote the exploit in Python, and Unit 42 saw agents try five techniques in sequence when earlier ones failed. In one AWS case it switched to IMDSv2 token acquisition after IMDSv1 was blocked and enumerated 20 services across eight regions; in another it pushed web shells to more than 40 locations on a Windows IIS server within 26 minutes, and Unit 42 describes another agent that found a vendor's GitLab credentials on a victim's web server and tried to brute-force access to that GitLab instance, exploiting no GitLab flaw (Unit 42, 2026-11-24).

    Unit 42 found no behavioural marker tying the activity to a model or framework; a command-and-control address in one campaign also hosted a CyberStrikeAI instance, from which it infers the orchestration platform (Unit 42, 2026-11-24). ReliaQuest saw a credential-gated CyberStrikeAI login page on a staging host and could not determine whether the tool played a part (ReliaQuest, 2026-10-07).

    Exposure
    Internet-facing web servers that host a vulnerable web application; Unit 42 does not name the applications.
    Detection
    In process-lineage telemetry, shells and scripts spawned through a web shell's sh -c interface in bursts seconds apart, with ===[Title]=== headers or reasoning-style comments and many unique Base64 payloads; the same enumeration command across hosts excluding security-product directories, and tracing of the endpoint-security daemon; on cloud workloads, a switch to IMDSv2 token requests after IMDSv1 is blocked.

    Triage: Base64 commands and reconnaissance appear in human-operated intrusions; Unit 42's discriminators are the volume and uniqueness of payloads, the command cadence and the headers, which it says rarely appear in human-operated attacks except with pre-coded scripts.

    Defender takeaway
    Hunt web-server hosts for these traces, looking for volumes of single-use scripts rather than malware families, and close the web-tier entry first; enforce MFA, sign-in rate limiting and source-address restriction on services whose credentials sit on web servers, as Unit 42 advises for GitLab.

    GeoServer 3.0.2, 2.28.6 and 2.27.7: a five-step chain from an anonymous WFS request to code execution (CVE-2026-129244, CVE-2026-129243, CVE-2026-120705, CVE-2026-129245, CVE-2026-129242) works on a default installation, was disclosed in advisories published on 2026-11-23 and has a public proof of concept; CISA lists CVE-2026-120705 and CVE-2026-129243 as exploited, and watchTowr reports exploitation attempts, which NCSC Switzerland relays as actively exploited (CVSS 4.0 9.2 for the critical step)

    The GeoServer project published a batch of security advisories on 2026-11-23, most for flaws fixed in 3.0.2, 2.28.6 and 2.27.7, which the project had announced on 2026-10-19 as an important upgrade for production systems (GeoServer, 2026-11-23; GeoServer, 2026-10-19). Five chain, in the order the project gives: a WFS stored-query exception that discloses the data directory path (CVE-2026-129244), service-security rules bypassed with a prefixed service name such as x/WFS so anonymous users can create stored queries (CVE-2026-129243), an unauthenticated WFS GetFeatureById XML injection (CVE-2026-120705), a predictable remember-me key that allows administrator session forgery (CVE-2026-129245, critical) and deserialization in the JDBC user and role services (CVE-2026-129242, administrator required) (GeoServer, 2026-11-23; GeoServer, 2026-11-23). The project says the critical advisory was first reported as step 4 of an unauthenticated remote command execution chain and that each step was given its own CVE at the CVE Numbering Authority's request (GeoServer, 2026-11-23).

    The reporter says the chain gives unauthenticated remote command execution on a default installation: the service-name bypass lets an anonymous stored query read the files that hold the remember-me key and an administrator password digest, which forges an administrator session (CryptoCat, 2026-11-23). A full-chain proof of concept has been public since the reporter's write-up. Affected are 3.0.0 to 3.0.1, 2.28.0 to 2.28.5 and 2.27.6 and earlier, with 3.0.2, 2.28.6 and 2.27.7 as the fixed releases (GeoServer, 2026-11-23); the project says the 2.27 series has reached end of life (GeoServer, 2026-10-19). CISA lists CVE-2026-120705 and CVE-2026-129243 as exploited, and watchTowr reports exploitation attempts, which NCSC Switzerland rated actively exploited on 2026-11-25 (CISA, 2026-11-27; NCSC Switzerland, 2026-11-25).

    Exposure
    GeoServer on the affected builds with WFS reachable by anonymous users and the REST API reachable; the reporter says it works on a default installation, with the stock admin account enabled in the default user service and, for its payload, a Linux host, and that the chain read the live key, so it never depended on the shipped value and a rotated key alone does not close it (CryptoCat, 2026-11-23); the fixed releases use a random per-node key and invalidate existing remember-me cookies on upgrade (GeoServer, 2026-11-23).
    Detection
    In web access logs, WFS requests whose service parameter contains a slash such as x/WFS, and stored queries you did not create under the data directory's wfs/query and workspaces/*/wfs/query folders (GeoServer, 2026-11-23); in REST access logs, an administrator session that uploads a database file and creates a JDBC user group service (GeoServer, 2026-11-23). If exposure is suspected the project advises changing administrator passwords, because changing the key does not help when the same file disclosure also reveals it (GeoServer, 2026-11-23).
    Defender takeaway
    Update internet-reachable GeoServer to a fixed release now rather than at the next cycle, since a public full-chain proof of concept exists, CISA lists two of the chain's flaws as exploited and the fixes are weeks old; where it must wait, apply the project's workarounds for steps 2 and 4, and on any instance that ran an older build while reachable, review the stored-query folders and the administrator accounts.
