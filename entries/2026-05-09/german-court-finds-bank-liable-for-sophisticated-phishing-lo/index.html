    Berlin court orders Apobank to refund EUR 218,000 lost to a letter, QR-code and phone scam that enrolled the attackers' own device

    A German court found a phishing victim not grossly negligent after a bank-branded letter, a cloned portal and a call led her to enrol the attackers' device

    first published 2026-05-09 05:00 UTC

    Analysis

    Landgericht Berlin II ruled on 2026-04-22 (38 O 293/25) that Apobank may not refuse to refund about EUR 218,000 on grounds of gross negligence: a bank-branded letter asked the customer to register a new security procedure, its QR code led to a cloned banking portal, a caller posing as the bank guided her through the process, and photographing a QR code let the attackers enrol their own device; the judgment is not yet final and the customer bears only the EUR 50 statutory share (Borncity, 2026-05-09). Beyond the holding, the chamber remarked that the login and the attackers' new-device registration came from entirely different IP addresses and providers and that the bank did not block this discrepancy automatically (heise online, 2026-05-08).

    Contradiction: Borncity has a QR code in the letter leading to the manipulated page (Borncity, 2026-05-09), while heise reports that the customer was asked to set up two-factor authentication during her usual login through her browser's favourites bar (heise online, 2026-05-08).

    Defender takeaway
    The attack's decisive step was a device enrolment authorised by the victim, so the control point is the enrolment itself: a new authenticator registered from a network or provider different from the session that requested it deserves a hold and an out-of-band confirmation (heise online, 2026-05-08).

    Updates1

    Correction11 Oct 2026 14:33 UTC

    The court's holding is that the customer was not grossly negligent, so the bank must refund the loss (Borncity, 2026-05-09); its comments on the IP and provider mismatch were remarks beyond the decision, which the claimant's lawyer called technically still amateurish (heise online, 2026-05-08). No cited source mentions PSD2 or a duty to trigger strong customer authentication, and the judgment is described as not yet final rather than under appeal.
