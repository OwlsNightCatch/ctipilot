    Phishing and BEC through hijacked Amazon SES accounts: leaked IAM keys let attackers send mail that passes SPF, DKIM and DMARC

    Kaspersky: attackers use leaked AWS IAM keys to send phishing and fabricated-thread BEC through legitimate Amazon SES accounts

    first published 2026-05-08 05:00 UTC

    Analysis

    Kaspersky reports that phishing sent through Amazon Simple Email Service (SES) rose in January 2026 and stayed steady through the first quarter (Kaspersky Securelist, 2026-05-04). Attackers mostly reach SES through leaked AWS IAM access keys that developers leave in public GitHub repositories, ENV files, Docker images, configuration backups or publicly accessible S3 buckets; bots built on the open-source secret scanner TruffleHog find the keys, and the attackers then check each key's permissions and sending limits (Kaspersky Securelist, 2026-05-04). Because the mail is sent from legitimate infrastructure, it passes SPF, DKIM and DMARC, almost always carries .amazonses.com in its Message-ID header, and comes from sender IPs that reputation blocklists cannot block without blocking all SES mail (Kaspersky Securelist, 2026-05-04).

    A common early-2026 lure was a fake electronic-signature notification whose link led to a sign-in form hosted on amazonaws.com (Kaspersky Securelist, 2026-05-04). In a business-email-compromise case Kaspersky investigated, an email posing as an employee went to the company's finance department with a fabricated thread about an outstanding invoice and PDF attachments holding only payment details and supporting documentation, with no malicious link or QR code (Kaspersky Securelist, 2026-05-04). BleepingComputer reported the research the same day (BleepingComputer, 2026-05-04).

    Exposure
    Any AWS account whose IAM access keys have leaked into a public repository, container image, backup or bucket can be turned into a phishing sender; an organization is exposed both as the owner of such keys and as a recipient of SES-sent mail (Kaspersky Securelist, 2026-05-04).
    Detection
    In mail-gateway telemetry, SES-sent messages almost always carry .amazonses.com in the Message-ID header; a message from an unfamiliar SES sender whose link resolves to a sign-in form on amazonaws.com, or an invoice thread to finance with PDFs holding only payment details and supporting documentation, is the pattern Kaspersky describes (Kaspersky Securelist, 2026-05-04). On the AWS side, in analyst judgement, SES sending activity by an IAM key that has no history of sending mail is the signal for a hijacked key.
    Defender takeaway
    Authentication results do not separate this mail from legitimate SES mail, so payment-change and invoice requests need out-of-band verification regardless of a passing DMARC result. For your own AWS accounts, Kaspersky advises least-privilege IAM keys, roles instead of long-lived keys, MFA, IP restrictions and automated key rotation with regular audits (Kaspersky Securelist, 2026-05-04).
