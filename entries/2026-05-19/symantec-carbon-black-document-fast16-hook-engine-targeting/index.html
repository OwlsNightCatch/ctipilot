    Symantec and Carbon Black: the fast16 sabotage framework corrupted LS-DYNA and AUTODYN nuclear-detonation simulations; Kim Zetter argues it was contemporaneous with Stuxnet

    Symantec: fast16 corrupted LS-DYNA and AUTODYN simulations of uranium compression in nuclear detonations

    first published 2026-05-19 05:00 UTC

    Analysis

    Broadcom's Symantec and Carbon Black teams published on 2026-05-16 an analysis of fast16, the sabotage framework that SentinelOne first analyzed in April 2026 and whose oldest components date from around 2005, and confirm that its hook engine targets LS-DYNA and AUTODYN, applications used to simulate problems such as vehicle crashworthiness, material modelling and explosive simulation (Broadcom Security, 2026-05-16; The Hacker News, 2026-05-18). The framework combines a service binary that embeds a Lua 5.0 virtual machine, a boot-start filesystem filter driver that patches Intel-compiled executables on the fly as they are read from disk, and 101 byte-pattern rules in nine or ten groups for different builds of the two programs (Broadcom Security, 2026-05-16). Its hooks act only in high-explosive simulations and scale down outputs such as pressure or stress once the simulated material density passes 30 g/cm³, the threshold Broadcom says uranium reaches only under the shock compression of an implosion device, which it reads as sabotage of nuclear weapons research (Broadcom Security, 2026-05-16).

    Before installing, fast16 checks for 18 endpoint-security registry keys and refuses to spread if any is present; it then registers itself as a service whose file takes the creation dates and permissions of a system binary, loads its driver as a SCSI-class filter at the next boot, and persists by hijacking a chosen application through the Image File Execution Options Debugger value (Broadcom Security, 2026-05-16). It spreads only inside its own network, enumerating domains, servers and shares and, for hosts in local address ranges, copying itself to their admin share under the logged-on user's credentials and creating a remote service (Broadcom Security, 2026-05-16). Kim Zetter, citing Symantec researchers and nuclear experts, argues that if fast16 targeted Iran in 2005 it was contemporaneous with Stuxnet rather than its predecessor, and that it fed false simulation data to engineers rather than altering weapons (Kim Zetter / ZERO DAY, 2026-05-16); Broadcom still calls it a pre-Stuxnet tool (Broadcom Security, 2026-05-16).

    Exposure
    Windows hosts that run Intel-compiled simulation software, specifically builds of LS-DYNA and AUTODYN from that era; the framework does not spread to hosts carrying any of the endpoint-security products it checks for (Broadcom Security, 2026-05-16).
    Detection
    In driver inventory telemetry, a boot-start filter driver registered under the SCSI class that is unsigned or unfamiliar; in registry telemetry, an Image File Execution Options Debugger value that launches a non-debugger binary and is deleted and re-added around each launch of the target application; in file and service telemetry, a new service binary whose timestamps match a system file; in network telemetry, one host copying an executable to the admin shares of same-subnet hosts and starting remote services (Broadcom Security, 2026-05-16).
    Defender takeaway
    Broadcom does not know whether a modern version of fast16 exists; operators of simulation and research-computing estates concerned about similar sabotage should inventory loaded drivers regularly, flagging unsigned or unfamiliar ones, and run tightly tuned application control (Broadcom Security, 2026-05-16). Other organizations have no decision to make.

    SentinelOne's first public analysis of fast16 appeared in April 2026 and dated its oldest components to around 2005, about two years before Stuxnet first became active (Broadcom Security, 2026-05-16); no source places that disclosure at LABScon or reports earlier speculation that fast16 attacked centrifuges. The two framings are not settled: Kim Zetter argues that if fast16 targeted Iran in 2005 it was contemporaneous with Stuxnet rather than its predecessor (Kim Zetter / ZERO DAY, 2026-05-16), while Broadcom's analysis, published on 2026-05-16 rather than 2026-05-18, still calls it a pre-Stuxnet tool (Broadcom Security, 2026-05-16). LS-DYNA and AUTODYN are applications for simulating problems such as vehicle crashworthiness, material modelling and explosive simulation, not weapons-design codes as such, and Broadcom does not claim the hook engine is the first documented technique of its kind; the detection it supports rests on the install artifacts it documents and on inventorying unsigned or unfamiliar drivers (Broadcom Security, 2026-05-16).
