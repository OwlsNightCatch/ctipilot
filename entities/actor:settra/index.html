    What each entry about Settra tells a defender to do, newest first.

    2026-11-11NOTABLEInsikt: GhostFrame implant re-disables ESXi execInstalledOnly every five seconds and steals guest credentials

    Defender takeaway

    Treat a VPN-appliance compromise as a possible hypervisor compromise and check the ESXi persistence files and the execInstalledOnly state before declaring the incident closed; restrict ESXi and vCenter management to dedicated administration hosts.

    Exposure · triage · detection
    Exposure

    ESXi hosts whose root credentials, SSH or shell, or vCenter are reachable after an edge compromise; no ESXi vulnerability is involved, so patching ESXi does not address it, and an estate that runs both a SonicWall, Palo Alto Networks or Fortinet VPN appliance and ESXi without isolated hypervisor management is in the pattern Insikt describes.

    Triage

    Administrators legitimately edit local.sh, create snapshots and use VNC; the discriminators are a firewall-off and execInstalledOnly loop, a binary under /opt named vmhostd where the genuine daemon is vmware-hostd, and snapshot-file reads by a non-backup account.

    Detection

    In ESXi file-integrity and shell-audit telemetry, changes to local.sh, a process masquerading as the host daemon or an unexpected executable under /opt, and repeated firewall-off and execInstalledOnly resets; in network telemetry, six near-simultaneous SYNs from the hypervisor management address to one destination on those ports, and any outbound command-style traffic from a hypervisor at all; in vCenter and ESXi logs, snapshot creation or deletion, VNC enablement and power-state changes outside change windows; in datastore telemetry, reads of VM memory-snapshot and virtual-disk files by an identity that is not the backup service.

    2026-08-20NOTABLEThe tell is the timing: a recovery offer that arrives while the intrusion is still private is foreknowledge, not marketing

    Defender takeaway

    During a ransomware incident, unsolicited inbound contact is part of the incident and belongs in the evidence, not in the inbox of whoever received it. A message offering to recover or delete data for a fee, arriving before anything is public, is a claim of foreknowledge that only a participant can honestly make, so it should be preserved, routed to the incident lead and treated as adversarial communication, not evaluated as a vendor proposal by an executive acting alone. Paying it buys nothing that can be verified: GuidePoint's own guidance is that no criminal party's promise to delete stolen data can be relied on, and retention for later re-extortion is a documented pattern.

    Triage
    Triage

    Legitimate recovery and negotiation firms are retained by the victim, normally through counsel or an existing incident-response contract, and they approach victims (if at all) only once an incident is public; the discriminators are the direction of first contact, the pre-disclosure timing, and a claim of standing access to the attacker's own infrastructure, which no lawful firm would advertise. For hunt teams, GuidePoint's reasoning is reusable in its own right: the co-occurrence of an internal network scanner, a cloud-storage exfiltration utility, a script-installed remote-management agent and a freshly created local account across two nominally unrelated ransomware cases is a signal that the same operator worked both, whatever brand claimed the victims.

    NOTABLENATOB2

    GhostFrame: Recorded Future's Insikt Group finds an ESXi post-exploitation framework (vmhostd-core implant, VM control panel, VMkatz credential theft) used by TAG-218, whose activity overlaps Settra ransomware and which targeted SonicWall, Palo Alto Networks and Fortinet VPN appliances in the US and Europe

    Insikt Group describes GhostFrame, a previously undocumented command-and-control and post-exploitation framework for compromised VMware ESXi hosts, which it saw used by TAG-218, a group "whose operations overlap with Settra ransomware" (Recorded Future, 2026-11-10). Operators must already hold root on the host: the implant, an ELF named vmhostd-core generated from a web operator panel, does not exploit a vulnerability to gain access (Recorded Future, 2026-11-10). It copies itself to /opt/vmhostd-core, edits the ESXi boot script /etc/rc.local.d/local.sh and runs /sbin/auto-backup.sh to keep the change across reboots, masquerades as vmhostd (resembling VMware's vmware-hostd), and about every five seconds disables the ESXi firewall and the execInstalledOnly control that restricts execution of unsigned binaries, so an administrator who re-enables them is undone (Recorded Future, 2026-11-10). Its command channel opens simultaneous TCP connections to one address on ports 53, 2222, 3389, 4444, 8443 and 9443, keeps the first that succeeds and retries after a random delay of 5 to 12 seconds, which Insikt notes is a network detection opportunity; the implant is mainly an in-memory shellcode loader with a reverse-shell fallback.

    From the hypervisor the panel can list registered VMs, change power state, create or delete snapshots, enable VNC on a guest, run a SOCKS5 proxy for pivoting, intercept ESXi logins, and run the open-source VMkatz against VM memory snapshots, virtual disks or whole datastores to recover NTLM hashes, Kerberos tickets, SAM and LSA secrets and NTDS.dit data without running anything inside the guest (Recorded Future, 2026-11-10). Insikt ties TAG-218's victim-facing servers to VPN-appliance targeting across the US and Europe between late August and October 2026, "predominantly SonicWall, Palo Alto Networks, and Fortinet devices", and says four of those organisations later appeared on the Settra leak site; it assesses that other financially motivated actors are highly likely to adopt the framework, which is an assessment, not an observation (Recorded Future, 2026-11-10). Unit 42 independently reports that after CVE-2026-0257 exploitation attackers pivot from the VPN gateway directly to the virtualization layer (Palo Alto Networks Unit 42, 2026-10-20).

    Exposure
    ESXi hosts whose root credentials, SSH or shell, or vCenter are reachable after an edge compromise; no ESXi vulnerability is involved, so patching ESXi does not address it, and an estate that runs both a SonicWall, Palo Alto Networks or Fortinet VPN appliance and ESXi without isolated hypervisor management is in the pattern Insikt describes.
    Detection
    In ESXi file-integrity and shell-audit telemetry, changes to local.sh, a process masquerading as the host daemon or an unexpected executable under /opt, and repeated firewall-off and execInstalledOnly resets; in network telemetry, six near-simultaneous SYNs from the hypervisor management address to one destination on those ports, and any outbound command-style traffic from a hypervisor at all; in vCenter and ESXi logs, snapshot creation or deletion, VNC enablement and power-state changes outside change windows; in datastore telemetry, reads of VM memory-snapshot and virtual-disk files by an identity that is not the backup service.

    Triage: administrators legitimately edit local.sh, create snapshots and use VNC; the discriminators are a firewall-off and execInstalledOnly loop, a binary under /opt named vmhostd where the genuine daemon is vmware-hostd, and snapshot-file reads by a non-backup account.

    Defender takeaway
    Treat a VPN-appliance compromise as a possible hypervisor compromise and check the ESXi persistence files and the execInstalledOnly state before declaring the incident closed; restrict ESXi and vCenter management to dedicated administration hosts.

    NOTABLENATOB2

    "Ransom Busters" emails ransomware victims before their incident is public, offering to delete the stolen data for a fee, and the tooling says it is the same affiliate who took it

    An entity styling itself Ransom Busters has been emailing organisations in the middle of a ransomware incident, writing to addresses at the victim's own domain and asking to be put in touch with the chief executive or IT leadership (GuidePoint Security, 2026-08-18). It presents itself as a project that assists victims of cyberattacks, claims to have held unauthorised access to criminal groups' servers for more than three years, says it has found the victim's stolen data on one of those servers, and offers to return the files, destroy the attackers' backups and (because it claims access to the operation's key storage) help restore encrypted data. The fee is $20,000 to $60,000 to delete the stolen data from the ransomware group's servers.

    The property that separates this from ordinary ambulance-chasing is when it arrives. GuidePoint notes that legitimate recovery-service solicitation generally follows an attack becoming public knowledge; this outreach lands before the incident is publicly known at all, which means the sender knew about an intrusion nobody had disclosed (GuidePoint Security, 2026-08-18). Responding to two of these incidents, GuidePoint's teams found the same set of artefacts in both victim environments: one network-scanning utility used for internal reconnaissance, one cloud-object-storage command-line tool used to move data out to attacker-controlled cloud storage, one remote-monitoring-and-management tool installed through a PowerShell script as a secondary access channel, a locally created backdoor account whose password was identical in both intrusions, and an identical attacker-controlled workstation name appearing in both. GuidePoint weighed and rejected the obvious innocent explanation (a standardised affiliate playbook or a shared virtual-machine image distributed inside one programme) because the same overlap recurred across incidents belonging to different ransomware operations, which makes it a fingerprint of an operator rather than of a programme. On that basis it assesses with moderate confidence that Ransom Busters is a single affiliate employed across several operations, using affiliate-level access to divert ransom negotiations away from the operation it is working for, and states it observed the behaviour while responding to incidents involving DragonForce, Settra and Anubis (GuidePoint Security, 2026-08-18).

    A second incident-response firm has seen the same thing independently. Coveware confirmed to BleepingComputer that it responded to at least one incident involving contact from the same group or individual, and its senior director of incident response drew the same distinction, that what makes this different from a routine recovery-service pitch is the approach to a victim whose incident was not yet public (BleepingComputer, 2026-08-19). No victim is reported to have paid Ransom Busters; in one incident the victim paid the underlying ransomware operator instead.

    Defender takeaway
    During a ransomware incident, unsolicited inbound contact is part of the incident and belongs in the evidence, not in the inbox of whoever received it. A message offering to recover or delete data for a fee, arriving before anything is public, is a claim of foreknowledge that only a participant can honestly make, so it should be preserved, routed to the incident lead and treated as adversarial communication, not evaluated as a vendor proposal by an executive acting alone. Paying it buys nothing that can be verified: GuidePoint's own guidance is that no criminal party's promise to delete stolen data can be relied on, and retention for later re-extortion is a documented pattern. Triage: legitimate recovery and negotiation firms are retained by the victim, normally through counsel or an existing incident-response contract, and they approach victims (if at all) only once an incident is public; the discriminators are the direction of first contact, the pre-disclosure timing, and a claim of standing access to the attacker's own infrastructure, which no lawful firm would advertise. For hunt teams, GuidePoint's reasoning is reusable in its own right: the co-occurrence of an internal network scanner, a cloud-storage exfiltration utility, a script-installed remote-management agent and a freshly created local account across two nominally unrelated ransomware cases is a signal that the same operator worked both, whatever brand claimed the victims.
