The first German cases followed on 2026-11-02: heise reports that the cold rooms of an Edeka market in Reisbach, Lower Bavaria, were heated to 50 degrees overnight into Monday and that the police have not confirmed a cyberattack as the cause, and WDR quotes a division manager at a CAP supermarket in North Rhine-Westphalia saying the actual attack hit the firm that maintains the cooling plants remotely, which is the operator's statement and not an authority finding (translated from German) ([heise, 2026-11-03](https://www.heise.de/news/Cyberangriffe-auf-Kuehlschraenke-Erste-deutsche-Faelle-in-Bayern-und-NRW-11508922.html); [WDR, 2026-11-03](https://www1.wdr.de/nrw/cyberangriffe-supermarkt-kuehltruhen-nrw-100.html)). SentinelLABS' assessment of 2026-11-03 says responsibility for the European incidents has not been publicly established, calls Russian-aligned disruptive groups one credible hypothesis based on precedent, and says shared maintenance access deserves scrutiny ([SentinelLABS, 2026-11-03](https://www.sentinelone.com/labs/defrost-and-disrupt-an-assessment-of-europe-s-refrigeration-attacks/)). Le Canard enchaîné reports that Coolworld, a company that maintains refrigerated storage containers for the French blood service, was hacked overnight on 1 to 2 November and that cold rooms held at minus 30 degrees were programmed to reach 110 degrees; the blood service says an on-duty technician saw the temperature rise and acknowledges that frozen plasma intended for transfusion was among the products, and the Health Ministry says there was zero operational consequence because the situation was spotted (translated from French) ([Le Canard enchaîné, 2026-11-03](https://www.lecanardenchaine.fr/police-justice/55332-les-hackers-ont-du-sang-sur-les-mains)). The 110 degrees is the programmed set-point and not a measured temperature, and no source names the equipment, the vendor or the access method, or ties this case to the Danfoss and Copeland controllers.
