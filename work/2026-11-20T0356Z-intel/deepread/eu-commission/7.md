A cyberattack on the European Commission disrupted staff services for more than 10 days, leaving some employees unable to submit timesheets, request leave, or enter salary information.

The EU’s cybersecurity service, CERT-EU, found that hackers used an [AI tool called ARTEX](https://cybersecuritynews.com/fbi-reveals-hackers-artex-ai-usage/), POLITICO reported on November 19, 2026. Most affected applications are back online, but investigators have not established whether anyone accessed or stole staff data.

According to an EU official who spoke to POLITICO, CERT-EU detected ARTEX’s use earlier in November. The publicly available tool uses artificial intelligence to look for security weaknesses in IT systems.

The report did not name the attackers, identify a specific flaw or explain how they gained access. It also did not establish whether ARTEX helped them break in or was used only to look for weaknesses.

The Commission took several applications offline as a precaution. Spokesperson Thomas Regnier said individual systems were targeted and that the Commission had informed those concerned. An EU official said the Commission believed it had contained most of the attack, but the full extent of the damage remained unclear.

## **Sysper, MyPMO and Translation Services Disrupted**

Two affected platforms were Sysper, which staff uses to view payslips, record work hours and request leave, and MyPMO, which handles medical reimbursements. Both returned earlier in the week, although Sysper briefly went down again on November 18.

Translation services were also disrupted on November 17. Salaries largely arrived as usual, POLITICO found, even though some staff could not complete routine HR tasks.

The outages show which services staff could not use, not which records the attackers may have seen. The Commission has not confirmed that payslips, leave records or medical reimbursement data were exposed.

An [EU official told POLITICO](https://www.politico.eu/newsletter/brussels-playbook/cyberattack-hits-the-commission/) that uncertainty about whether personal information was compromised, and how much, was one reason staff had received limited details.

The Corporate Management Board, the Commission’s top internal crisis group, held an urgent meeting earlier in November, according to an internal email reviewed by POLITICO.

The Commission’s digital services department ordered extra security checks, and technology commissioner Henna Virkkunen briefed fellow commissioners on November 18. Reuters, citing the original report, said the Commission did not immediately respond to its separate request for comment.

The incident follows two separate cases covered by Cyber Security News. In January, an attack on the [system that manages Commission staff mobile devices](https://cybersecuritynews.com/european-commission-cyber-attack/) exposed a limited set of names and phone numbers. In March, a [cloud breach tied to the Trivy supply-chain attack](https://cybersecuritynews.com/european-commission-breach-trivy/) affected the public Europa website platform. CERT-EU said the March breach did not affect the Commission’s internal systems.

Neither earlier case has been publicly linked to the November attack. For staff, getting Sysper and MyPMO back means they can resume routine work.

It does not answer the central question of the investigation: whether attackers reached personal records and, if they did, which employees and types of data were affected.
